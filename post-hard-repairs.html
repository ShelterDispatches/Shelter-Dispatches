Hard Repairs at Scale:
Why Category 2 Can't Wait
Any Longer

·
📍 Kharkiv Oblast
·
Category: Structural Repairs

The rafter frame is exposed to open sky. The internal wall has collapsed inward, leaving a gap you could walk through. Yellow insulation wool hangs from what used to be a ceiling like the remnants of something that once tried to keep people warm. This is not a house that needs new windows. This is a house that needs an engineer, a structural calculation, a contractor with a crane, and a budget that most humanitarian shelter programmes are not designed to provide.

Three years into the response, the picture across frontline oblasts has shifted significantly. The volume of Light and Medium repair requests — the bread and butter of Category 1 shelter programming — is declining. Not because the need has disappeared, but because the surface layer of repairable damage has been largely absorbed by successive programme cycles since 2022. What remains is harder. More expensive. Structurally more complex. And it is accelerating.

Interior assessment — collapsed load-bearing wall, exposed rafter frame, open roof. This household falls well outside Category 1 scope. Kharkiv Oblast, field visit 2026.

What the SOP Says — and What the Field Shows

The Shelter Cluster SOP (February 2026) draws a clear line between Category 1 and what lies beyond it. Category 1 — Light and Medium repairs — addresses non-structural damage: broken windows, damaged roofing panels, minor interior works. The envelope is closed, minimum habitability is restored, and the household can return. Average cost: $2,000–$8,600 depending on household size and repair type.

Category 2 begins where load-bearing capacity is compromised. Foundations cracked by blast pressure. Walls that have lost structural integrity. Floor plates that have partially collapsed. Roof structures — not just roof coverings — that have been destroyed. These are buildings where a contractor who shows up with windows and insulation has no business being there.

SOP Definition — Category 2 Threshold

Structural damage is defined as damage to load-bearing elements — foundations, load-bearing walls, floor structures, columns, beams — or to critical engineering systems that create a safety hazard for residents. This is explicitly outside the scope of Category 1 humanitarian repairs and requires engineering assessment, project design, and specialised contractor capacity.

The problem is that this definition has always been clean on paper and messy in the field. Engineers conducting initial assessments must make a call — Category 1 or not — often under time pressure, sometimes with incomplete access to the structure. The grey zone is wide. And the consequence of a wrong call in either direction is significant: underclassify, and a family returns to a structurally unsafe home; overclassify, and a repairable house joins a Category 2 backlog that nobody is funded to address.

What is happening in practice is a sorting problem. Households that were assessed, identified as requiring structural intervention, and found ineligible for Category 1 programmes have been placed in a holding pattern. Local authorities are aware of them. Cluster partners are aware of them. But there is no systematic, funded Category 2 response at scale — and the gap between what the state can provide through eVidnovlennia and what these households actually need is not narrowing.

The easy work is done. What's left requires a completely different set of tools — and a completely different budget.

What a Category 2 Response Needs to Look Like

The most significant operational difference between Category 1 and Category 2 is not just cost — it is complexity. A Category 2 intervention requires a technical project design before a single brick is touched. This means an engineering assessment that goes beyond visual inspection, a structural calculation, a Bill of Quantities that reflects actual construction methodology, and — critically — a contractor with the specialised capacity to execute it safely.

External assessment view — roof structure destroyed, debris across property. This household requires structural engineering assessment before any repair scope can be determined. Kharkiv Oblast, field visit 2026. Identity of field staff anonymised.

One response model that is gaining traction is the Framework Agreement approach: pre-qualifying a contractor not just to execute works, but to produce the technical design as part of the framework. This collapses the time between assessment and works commencement — instead of the standard sequence of assessment → design procurement → tender → execution, the contractor who does the design is already on contract to execute. For households who have been waiting two years for someone to tell them whether their house can be fixed, this matters.

Local Authority Demand — A Signal That Cannot Be Ignored

Perhaps the clearest indicator of where the need is concentrating is the shift in where requests are coming from. Field teams are increasingly receiving Category 2 referrals not from individual households but from local authorities — hromadas and city councils who are tracking the structural damage caseload in their communities and actively looking for humanitarian partners who can address it.

This is significant. It means the demand is documented, aggregated, and coming with some degree of local institutional backing. It also means that local authorities are prepared to engage in the MoU frameworks and coordination mechanisms that a technically complex Category 2 programme requires. The alignment is there. The response capacity is not.

Field Observation — Kharkiv Oblast

During site visits in early 2026, local authority contacts in multiple hromadas across Kharkiv Oblast confirmed that they maintain informal registers of households with structural damage that falls outside current humanitarian programme parameters. In several cases, households had been on these lists since 2022 assessments. The common theme: assessed, documented, and waiting.

The Programme Logic for 2026–2027

Category 2 cannot continue to be the answer to a question nobody is funded to ask. The Shelter response in Ukraine is at a moment of necessary transition — from the high-volume, standardised repairs that characterised the first two years, to a more complex, technically demanding, and inevitably more expensive second phase. This transition requires a different kind of programme design: longer implementation timelines, engineer-led assessment processes, pre-qualified contractor frameworks, and donor flexibility on unit costs.

The households in these photographs are not edge cases. They are the leading edge of a caseload that is now the dominant unmet shelter need across frontline oblasts. The response needs to catch up.
